Ward 5 and 311: who reports, and who doesn't

Ward 5 is Calgary's largest ward and makes the fewest 311 infrastructure reports per resident. The video explains how much of that is expected, where the gap is (Taradale and Martindale), and why a lower number isn't automatically good news. Subtitles are built in.

Full details: Ward 5 page · Findings. Download the video (MP4, 10 MB). The ward diagram in the video is schematic, not a map.

Transcript

Calgary has one number for everything from potholes to broken streetlights: 311. But does every neighbourhood use it the same way?

Ward 5, in Calgary’s northeast, is the city’s largest ward. It’s home to about 134,000 people.

An independent study looked at more than 800,000 311 requests, across 195 communities, over two years.

Ward 5 makes the fewest infrastructure reports per resident of all 14 wards. About 37 per 1,000 residents a year, against 67 citywide.

Part of that is expected. Many Ward 5 communities are new, and new roads and pipes break less. But compared with similar communities, the ward still reports 8% less.

The gap is concentrated in two older communities. Taradale reports 32% less than similar communities, and Martindale 26% less. They also have the ward’s highest shares of residents who can’t hold a conversation in English.

The service itself is not the problem. Ward 5 requests are closed as fast as anywhere else. And residents who do report use the 311 app just as often as the rest of Calgary.

So a lower number isn’t automatically good news. It can mean problems are going unreported.

Growth vs amenities: have parks, playgrounds and transit kept up?

Calgary's communities have added about 300,000 residents since the 2021 Census. This video compares the 45 communities that grew fastest with the rest of the city on playgrounds, transit stops, park space and trees, explains how it's measured, and shows the 23 communities where amenities have not kept up with growth. Subtitles are built in.

Data: Calgary Data Desk (City of Calgary open data; amenities counted November 3, 2026; residents estimated November 2026). City assets only: school, private and community association amenities are not counted. Download the video (MP4, 13 MB).

Transcript

Calgary is growing fast. Since the 2021 Census, its communities have added about 300,000 residents. But have parks, playgrounds and transit stops kept up?

Calgary Data Desk counts the City’s own amenities in every community, from playgrounds and park space to trees and transit stops, and compares each one with the city as a whole.

Here’s how it’s measured. Residents today come from the City’s own population estimate, shared out by where new homes were actually built since 2021, using the property assessment roll. Growth compares that with the 2021 Census. Each amenity is counted per 1,000 residents, and playgrounds per 1,000 children, then compared with the city as a whole. A community’s amenity level averages those comparisons: 1.00 is the city level, and no single amenity counts more than double. Libraries, recreation centres and park washrooms serve several communities, so they’re left out of a community’s level. A community is flagged when it grew more than 20%, or is new since 2021, and its amenity level is below 0.75.

45 communities grew by more than 20%, or didn’t exist in 2021. Together they added about 166,000 people. That’s more than half of the city’s growth.

Per resident, those growing communities have less. About 2.7 playgrounds per 1,000 children, against 4.6 in the rest of Calgary. Two transit stops per 1,000 residents, against nearly four. About a quarter less local park space, and more than a third fewer trees.

23 communities grew fast and sit below three-quarters of the city’s amenity level. They are home to about 230,000 people, including more than 45,000 children.

The largest are the Beltline, Saddle Ridge, Mahogany, Skyview Ranch and Sage Hill. The fastest growing, Belmont, went from about 800 residents in 2021 to about 6,500 today.

These counts include City assets only. School, private and community association amenities aren’t counted, and new communities often get parks and facilities in later phases.
